For most of my working life, my job was to ask what could go wrong. Not in the abstract, anxious way we all do at three in the morning, but formally, with spreadsheets and committees and sign-offs. What happens to this portfolio if rates move two points? What if this counterparty fails? What if the thing everyone assumes will keep working simply stops?

It is a strange discipline to practise all day and then switch off when you look at your own life. But that is exactly what most of us do. We are rigorous about other people's exposure and sentimental about our own.

When I told people I was leaving finance to write, the most common reaction was some version of: *that's brave*. It was meant kindly. It was also, I think, quietly wrong. It assumed that the job I was leaving was the safe option and the thing I was moving towards was the risky one. That framing is so universal that it barely registers as a framing at all. It feels like a fact.

It isn't. It is a bet, and like most bets we make without noticing, it is badly priced.

## The risk you can see and the risk you can't

In risk work there is a useful distinction between risks that show up in the numbers and risks that don't. The first kind is volatile, visible and uncomfortable. Prices move, you see the loss, you feel it. The second kind is quiet. It accumulates in the background, looks like stability for years, and then arrives all at once.

Leaving a job is the first kind of risk. The downside is obvious and immediate: less income, less structure, the social cost of explaining yourself at dinner. You can model it on a napkin. That visibility is exactly why it feels so frightening.

Staying is the second kind. Nothing moves. Your salary arrives on the same day each month. The dashboard is green. But underneath, a few things are happening that never appear on any statement:

- Your skills are narrowing to fit one organisation's way of doing things.
- Your sense of what you could do is quietly shrinking to match what you currently do.
- The years in which change is cheapest are being spent, one uneventful quarter at a time.

None of this feels like loss, because none of it arrives as an event. It arrives as a mood. A slight flatness on Sunday evenings. A habit of saying "one more year" that has now been said five times.

## Why we underprice staying

If staying carries real risk, why does almost everyone treat it as the default? I think there are three reasons, and they are all very human.

The first is that we weigh losses we cause more heavily than losses we allow. If you leave and it goes badly, it is your fault, and everyone can see it. If you stay and slowly become someone you didn't intend to be, there is no single decision to point at. Nobody asks you to justify the job you didn't take or the thing you didn't build. Inaction comes with an alibi.

The second is that institutions are very good at making the path ahead look continuous. There is always a next title, a next band, a next review cycle. Each step is small and reasonable. The structure is designed, with the best intentions, to make leaving feel like stepping off a moving train rather than simply choosing a different one.

The third is that the people around you are mostly making the same bet. When your peers, your manager and your parents all agree that staying is prudent, it stops feeling like a position you have taken and starts feeling like reality. Consensus is comforting. It is also, as anyone who has watched a market knows, a poor substitute for analysis.

> The question is never whether a path is risky. Every path is risky. The question is whether you have chosen your risks or merely inherited them.

## What pricing it properly looks like

I don't think the answer is that everyone should quit. That would be its own kind of sentimentality, just pointed in the other direction. Plenty of people are in exactly the right job, and the stable path is the correct bet for them. The point is not to reverse the default. It is to stop treating it as a default at all.

In practice, pricing the bet honestly came down to a handful of questions I started asking myself. They are the same questions I would have asked of any position on a trading book.

**What is my real downside?** Not the catastrophic version I imagined at night, but the realistic one. For me, that meant writing down how many months I could live on savings, what I would do if the writing earned nothing for a year, and how easily I could return to consulting if I had to. The answer was: more easily than I feared. Most careers are far more reversible than they feel from the inside.

**What is the cost of waiting?** Every year I delayed, the switch got a little more expensive. Obligations grow. Lifestyle expands to fit income. The identity of "the finance person" hardens. Waiting is not free. It is a position with a carrying cost, and that cost compounds.

**What does the upside actually look like?** Not in fantasy terms, but concretely. What would a good year of writing look like? What skills would I have in three years that I don't have now? The upside of staying was easy to describe because I could see it in the people ten years ahead of me. The upside of leaving was harder to see precisely because no one ahead of me had walked that exact path. That uncertainty was not a reason to avoid it. It was the reason it was worth doing.

**Who am I becoming if nothing changes?** This was the question that settled it. I looked honestly at where the path led, and I didn't want to arrive there. Not because it was a bad destination, but because it wasn't mine.

## The part nobody tells you

The strangest thing about leaving was how quickly the fear changed shape. Before I left, the fear was about money and failure. Within a few weeks, it was about something else entirely: whether I was using the time well. That is a much better fear to have. It is the fear of someone who has chosen their bet and now has to play it properly.

I still don't know how this will turn out. That is the honest answer, and it's the one I give when people ask. But I know that I made the decision with my eyes open, having priced both sides, rather than letting the absence of a decision make it for me.

If you are reading this from inside a job that looks fine on paper and feels increasingly wrong in practice, I am not going to tell you to leave. I am going to suggest something smaller and harder: sit down and price the bet you are already making. Write down what staying costs you, in the currency that actually matters to you. Time, energy, curiosity, the version of yourself you keep postponing.

You may well conclude that staying is right. Many people do, and they are correct. But at least it will be a decision rather than a drift. And if you conclude the opposite, you will find that leaving is not the reckless leap it looked like from the other side.

It's just a different bet. One you chose.
