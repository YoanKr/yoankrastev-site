Early in my career, a senior colleague gave me a piece of advice that I repeated to others for years. "Don't specialise too early," he said. "Keep your options open. The best careers are built on optionality."

It sounded wise. It sounded like the kind of thing finance people say, and I was a finance person. Options, after all, are valuable. In markets, an option gives you the right but not the obligation to do something later, and people pay real money for that flexibility. It seemed obvious that the same logic applied to careers. Why commit when you could wait, keep learning, and decide once you knew more?

I now think this is one of the most quietly damaging ideas in professional life. Not because it's false, but because it's true in a way that is very easy to misuse.

## What an option actually costs

The thing people forget about financial options is that they are not free. You pay a premium to hold them. And they have an expiry date. An option you never exercise is not a sign of prudence. It is simply money you spent on flexibility you never used.

Careers work the same way. Keeping your options open has a premium, and you pay it in a currency that is easy to overlook: depth. Every year you spend preserving flexibility is a year you are not spending getting unusually good at one thing. And in most fields, the rewards go disproportionately to people who have gone deep.

The generalist who keeps every door open often discovers, a decade in, that the doors have quietly become less valuable. The roles that once looked attainable now want someone with ten years of focused experience. The pivot that was easy at 26 is harder at 36, not because of age, but because the person who stayed flexible never built the foundation that makes a pivot credible.

> An option you never exercise is not a sign of prudence. It is a premium paid for a flexibility you never used.

## Why optionality feels so good

If the costs are real, why is the idea so attractive? I think it's because keeping your options open protects you from something most ambitious people fear more than failure: being wrong about who they are.

To commit to a direction is to make a claim about yourself. *I am a writer. I am building this company. I am going to be excellent at this particular thing.* The moment you say it out loud, you create the possibility of falling short of it publicly. Optionality lets you avoid that exposure indefinitely. As long as you haven't chosen, you can't have chosen wrong.

Corporate life is very good at supporting this. Rotational programmes, broad job titles and the constant availability of the next internal move all make it possible to stay in a state of productive indecision for a remarkably long time. You are always developing, always gaining exposure, always building a profile. You are just never quite arriving anywhere.

I recognise this pattern because I lived it. For years I told myself I was keeping my options open, when in truth I was avoiding the one option I actually wanted, because exercising it meant admitting I wanted it.

## The difference between flexibility and drift

None of this means that flexibility is bad. There are periods of life when exploring widely is exactly right. Early on, you genuinely don't know enough about the world or yourself to commit well, and trying several things is the fastest way to find out.

The problem is not exploration. It's exploration without an end date. Here is the test I've come to use: **is this flexibility helping me make a decision, or helping me avoid one?**

Useful exploration has a question attached. *Do I enjoy client work more than internal strategy? Am I better at building models or presenting them?* You try something, you learn the answer, and the option space narrows. That is the whole point.

Drift has no question. It just has a vague sense that something better might appear if you wait a little longer and stay a little more available. The option space never narrows. It just gets older.

If you're unsure which one you're in, ask yourself what you've actually learned in the last two years that has changed what you want. If the honest answer is "not much", you're probably not exploring. You're holding an option and paying the premium.

## Exercising the option

The hardest part of any option is deciding when to exercise it. In finance there are models for this. In life there aren't, and the lack of a clean answer is exactly what keeps people waiting.

What helped me was reframing the question. I stopped asking *is this the right time?* and started asking *what would have to be true for me to act?* Then I wrote the answer down. For me, the list was surprisingly short:

- Enough savings to live modestly for eighteen months.
- Evidence, even small, that people wanted to read what I wrote.
- A realistic plan for what I'd do if it didn't work.

Once all three were true, I no longer had a reason to wait, only a feeling that I should. And I had learned by then to be suspicious of that feeling. It had been telling me to wait for years, and it would happily have kept telling me forever.

---

There's a line I think about often, from someone who had built something serious late in life. He said that the regret he felt wasn't for the bets that went wrong. It was for the years he spent "being ready". The options he held so carefully mostly expired unused.

I'm not arguing for recklessness. I spent too long in risk management to believe in that. I'm arguing for honesty about what flexibility is costing you, and for recognising that at some point, keeping every door open becomes its own kind of commitment. It's a commitment to the corridor.

Most of us, if we're honest, already know which door we'd walk through if we weren't afraid of being wrong about ourselves. The option has value. But its value comes from using it. At some point, you have to exercise it, or accept that you never really meant to.
